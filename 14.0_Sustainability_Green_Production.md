## 14.1 The Carbon Footprint of Post-Production and Color Management

### Industry Context

Film and television generate more CO2 than most people realize. Production gets the blame — flights, trucks, generators — but post-production and color management rack up serious numbers too:

- **Data centers** powering cloud rendering, storage, and remote grading can drain as much electricity as a small city.
- **Render farms** transcoding and delivering finals often run 24/7, burning hundreds of megawatt-hours per project.
- **Storage** piles up fast: RAW footage, VFX plates, IMF masters, and archival copies sit on spinning disks or cloud servers that need constant cooling.
- **Physical delivery** still happens — hard drives, LTO tapes, DCPs — and every shipment carries a manufacturing and transport footprint.
- **On-set power** adds up quietly. DIT carts, reference monitors, grading suites, and LED volumes all draw serious current.

- **Data centers:** Cloud rendering, cloud storage, and cloud-based color grading rely on data centers that consume enormous amounts of electricity. A single data center can consume as much power as a small city.
- **Rendering farms:** VFX rendering, color grading renders, and transcoding farms run 24/7 during post-production, consuming hundreds of megawatt-hours of electricity per project.
- **Storage:** Long-term archival of production footage (camera RAW, VFX plates, final masters, IMF packages) requires petabytes of storage, which consumes energy for both the storage media and the cooling systems.
- **Transport:** Physical media delivery (hard drives, LTO tapes, DCPs) requires manufacturing, packaging, and shipping, all of which have carbon footprints.
- **On-set energy:** DIT carts, on-set monitors, color grading suites, and viewing theaters draw a lot of power during production and post-production.

**Estimated Carbon Footprint by Phase (per feature film, $50M budget):**

| Production Phase | Estimated CO2e (tonnes) | % of Total |
|-----------------|------------------------|------------|
| **Production (travel, transport, on-set)** | 8,000–15,000 | 60–70% |
| **Post-production (VFX, color, sound)** | 2,000–4,000 | 15–25% |
| **Data storage and archival (10-year)** | 1,000–2,000 | 5–10% |
| **Distribution and delivery (DCP, streaming)** | 500–1,000 | 3–5% |
| **Marketing and promotion (travel, events)** | 1,000–2,000 | 5–10% |
| **Total** | **12,500–24,000** | **100%** |

*Note: These are rough estimates based on industry reports (BAFTA albert, Netflix Sustainability Report, AMPAS). The exact footprint varies a lot by production location, energy grid mix, and workflow choices.*

### Data Center Energy Consumption

**Cloud rendering for color grading and VFX:**
- A single cloud GPU instance (e.g., NVIDIA A100, 80GB VRAM) consumes approximately **250–400 watts** when fully utilized.
- A 40-hour grading week on a cloud A100 instance consumes **10–16 kWh** of electricity.
- A typical VFX render for a feature film (e.g., 1,500 VFX shots, 2K–4K resolution, 24–48 hours render time per shot) can consume **50,000–200,000 kWh** of cloud rendering energy.
- **Carbon intensity varies by region:**
  - **Iceland, Norway, Sweden:** Near-zero carbon intensity (hydroelectric/geothermal). Cloud rendering in these regions has minimal carbon impact.
  - **France:** Low carbon intensity (nuclear). ~50 g CO2/kWh.
  - **Germany:** Medium carbon intensity (mixed grid). ~300 g CO2/kWh.
  - **United States (average):** Medium–high carbon intensity. ~400 g CO2/kWh.
  - **China, India, Australia:** High carbon intensity (coal-heavy). ~500–800 g CO2/kWh.

**Recommendation:** If using cloud rendering, select cloud regions with low carbon intensity. AWS, Google Cloud, and Azure all provide carbon intensity data for their regions. For a European production, render in **Ireland** (AWS/Azure) or **Belgium** (Google) for low-carbon grid mix. For a US production, render in **Oregon** (AWS) or **Iowa** (Google) for renewable-heavy grids.

### On-Set Energy Consumption

**DIT cart and on-set monitoring:**
- A typical DIT cart with a Mac Pro, RAID array, waveform/vectorscope monitor, and reference monitor consumes **500–800 watts** continuously.
- A 12-hour shooting day consumes **6–9.6 kWh**.
- A 60-day shoot consumes **360–576 kWh** for the DIT cart alone.
- **Reference monitors:** A 24-inch OLED reference monitor consumes **150–250 watts**. A 55-inch consumer OLED consumes **100–200 watts**. Multiple monitors on set (DIT, director, DP, client) can add up to **500–1,000 watts**.
- **LED volumes:** Virtual production LED walls are extremely energy-intensive. A 100 m² LED wall (e.g., ROE Visual Black Pearl BP2V2) consumes approximately **15–25 kW** at full brightness. A 12-hour VP shoot day consumes **180–300 kWh** for the wall alone.

**Recommendation:**
- Use **energy-efficient monitors** (e.g., ASUS ProArt PA32UCXR with Mini LED backlight, which has lower power consumption than full-array OLED at equivalent brightness).
- Turn off monitors and DIT equipment during breaks, lunch, and setup changes. Do not leave equipment in standby — standby mode still consumes 10–20% of full power.
- For VP stages, reduce LED wall brightness to the minimum required for the scene (e.g., 500 nits for night scenes, 1,000 nits for day scenes) rather than running at maximum brightness (2,000+ nits) for all scenes. This reduces energy consumption by 50–75% and also extends LED panel lifespan.
- Use **natural lighting** where possible for non-VP scenes, reducing the need for artificial lighting and LED walls.

## 14.2 Energy-Efficient Codecs and Compression

### Codec Energy Efficiency Comparison

The choice of codec matters a lot for the energy consumption of encoding, decoding, storage, and transmission. The following table summarizes the energy efficiency of common codecs for production and delivery:

| Codec | Application | Encoding Energy (Relative) | Decoding Energy (Relative) | Storage Efficiency | Transmission Efficiency | Notes |
|-------|-------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|-------------------|------------------------|-------|
| **ProRes 4444 XQ** | Production master | Very High | Low | Very Low | Very Low | Uncompressed-like quality; highest energy/storage cost |
| **ProRes 422 HQ** | Production proxy | High | Low | Low | Low | Common mezzanine format; moderate energy cost |
| **DNxHR 444** | Production master | Very High | Low | Very Low | Very Low | Avid equivalent to ProRes 4444 XQ |
| **OpenEXR (16-bit half)** | VFX/CGI | High | Medium | Low | Low | Lossless/lossy compression options; efficient for VFX |
| **H.264** | Delivery / proxy | Low | Very Low | Medium | Medium | Widely supported; hardware decode on all devices |
| **HEVC (H.265)** | Delivery / UHD | Medium | Low | High | High | 50% bitrate savings vs. H.264; hardware decode common |
| **AV1** | Streaming delivery | High | Medium | Very High | Very High | 30% savings vs. HEVC; higher encoding energy |
| **VVC (H.266)** | Broadcast / streaming | Very High | Medium | Very High | Very High | 50% savings vs. HEVC; very high encoding energy |
| **LCEVC** | Enhancement layer | Low | Very Low | Medium | Medium | Low-complexity enhancement; adds 30–55% efficiency to base codec |
| **ProRes RAW** | Camera RAW | Medium | Medium | Low | Low | Compressed RAW; lower storage than uncompressed RAW |
| **REDCODE RAW** | Camera RAW | Medium | Medium | Low | Low | RED proprietary; efficient compression |
| **BRAW** | Camera RAW | Medium | Medium | Low | Low | Blackmagic RAW; efficient compression |
| **Sony X-OCN / X-OCN XT** | Camera RAW | Medium | Medium | Low | Low | Sony compressed RAW; efficient for VENICE |

**Key Insight:** The energy cost of encoding is **front-loaded** (once per master), while the energy cost of decoding and transmission is **repeated** (millions of times for popular content). For a Netflix Original viewed by 100 million households, the decoding energy across all viewers far exceeds the encoding energy. That means **more efficient codecs (AV1, VVC, HEVC) reduce the total lifecycle energy** despite higher encoding cost.

### Recommendations for Energy-Efficient Post-Production

1. **Production masters:** Use **ProRes 4444 XQ** or **DNxHR 444** for production masters (these are the standard for color fidelity). The energy cost is justified by the quality requirement. However, for proxy workflows, use **H.264** or **HEVC** proxies rather than ProRes Proxy, as H.264/HEVC hardware decoding is more energy-efficient.
2. **VFX plates:** Use **OpenEXR with DWA (DreamWorks Animation) compression** or **B44A/B44** compression. These are lossy compression schemes optimized for VFX (preserving float precision, supporting alpha channels) and are more storage-efficient than uncompressed EXR. The energy savings from reduced storage and network transfer outweigh the encoding cost.
3. **Delivery:** For streaming delivery, use **AV1** or **HEVC** (not H.264) for 4K+ content. The 30–50% bitrate reduction translates directly to reduced CDN energy consumption, data center energy, and end-user device energy. For SDR content below 1080p, H.264 is still efficient (most devices have hardware decode, and the bitrate savings of HEVC/AV1 are marginal at low resolutions).
4. **Archival:** For long-term archival (10+ years), use **LTO-9 tape** (18 TB per cartridge, ~30-year lifespan) rather than hard disk drives (HDDs) or SSDs. LTO tape consumes zero energy when not being accessed (it is offline storage), while HDDs consume 5–10 watts continuously. For a 500 TB archive, LTO-9 requires ~28 cartridges and consumes **zero idle energy**, while HDDs would consume **2,500–5,000 watts continuously** (~22,000–44,000 kWh/year).
5. **Cloud storage tiers:** Use **cold storage** (e.g., AWS Glacier, Azure Archive, Google Cloud Coldline) for archival footage that is rarely accessed. Cold storage consumes 80–90% less energy than hot storage (S3 Standard, Azure Hot Blob) because the data is stored on tape or low-power drives, and access latency is higher (minutes to hours). For production footage that is only accessed during post-production and then archived, cold storage is the appropriate tier.

### Green Production Standards and Certifications

**BAFTA albert** runs the UK's main sustainability standard for film and TV.

To certify, productions must calculate their carbon footprint (using the albert calculator), commit to sustainable travel, use renewable energy where possible, and cut waste. Post houses also report energy use and can earn an albert supplier mark.

**If you are a DIT on an albert-certified production:** document your cart's daily power draw — monitors, storage, computer — and feed that number to the sustainability coordinator.

**Amazon Studios, Disney, and the BBC** have similar mandates. The details vary, but the ask is the same: measure your footprint and have a plan to reduce it.

## 14.3 Practical Sustainability Recommendations for DITs and Post Houses

### On-Set DIT Practices

1. **Energy monitoring:** Use a **power meter** (e.g., Kill A Watt, Watts Up Pro) to measure the DIT cart's energy consumption. Document the daily consumption and report it to the production's sustainability coordinator.
2. **Efficient equipment selection:**
   - Choose **laptop-based DIT setups** (e.g., MacBook Pro M3 Max, Dell Precision 7780) over desktop workstations (Mac Pro, HP Z8) for mobile shoots. Laptops consume 50–100 watts vs. 300–500 watts for desktops.
   - Use **SSD-based RAID** instead of HDD-based RAID for on-set storage. SSDs consume less power, generate less heat, and are more reliable in challenging environments.
   - Use **energy-efficient monitors** (e.g., Mini LED or LCD with local dimming) instead of OLED for on-set monitoring. OLEDs consume more power at high brightness (which is often needed for outdoor shoots). For color-critical monitoring in a controlled environment, OLED is still preferred, but for on-set preview, Mini LED is more energy-efficient.
3. **Power management:**
   - Turn off all equipment during breaks, lunch, and setup changes. Do not use sleep mode — fully power off monitors, storage, and computers.
   - Use **smart power strips** that automatically cut power to peripherals when the main computer is turned off.
   - For multi-day shoots, shut down the DIT cart completely at the end of each day, rather than leaving it in sleep mode overnight.
4. **Digital delivery:**
   - Deliver dailies and rushes via **cloud platforms** (Frame.io, PIX, Sohonet) or **local network** (Wi-Fi 6E, 10GbE) instead of physical media (hard drives, USB sticks, DVDs).
   - If physical media is required (e.g., for a VFX vendor with no internet access), use **reusable hard drives** (e.g., Glyph, G-Technology) rather than disposable USB sticks. Label drives clearly and return them to the production for reuse.
   - For LTO archival, use **LTO-9** (18 TB per cartridge) rather than LTO-8 (12 TB) or LTO-7 (6 TB). Higher density reduces the number of cartridges, packaging, and shipping.
5. **Sustainable sourcing:**
   - Choose **refurbished or second-hand equipment** where possible (e.g., monitors, storage, cables). The carbon footprint of manufacturing new electronics is not trivial (e.g., a 24-inch monitor has a manufacturing footprint of ~200–300 kg CO2e).
   - Use **cable management** to reduce waste — reusable cable ties, labeled cables, and cable organizers prevent cable loss and damage.

### Post-Production Facility Practices

1. **Renewable energy:**
   - Switch to a **100% renewable energy tariff** (e.g., wind, solar, hydro) for the facility's electricity. In many regions, this is cost-competitive with fossil fuel electricity.
   - Install **on-site solar panels** if the facility owns the building. A 50 kW solar array can offset 50–80% of a post house's electricity consumption.
   - Purchase **renewable energy certificates (RECs)** or **carbon offsets** for unavoidable emissions (e.g., natural gas heating, employee commuting).
2. **Energy-efficient infrastructure:**
   - Upgrade to **energy-efficient cooling** (e.g., free cooling, liquid cooling, hot aisle/cold aisle containment) for server rooms and render farms. Cooling can account for 30–50% of a data center's energy consumption.
   - Use **variable speed drives (VSDs)** for fans and pumps, which reduce energy consumption by 20–40% compared to fixed-speed drives.
   - Install **LED lighting** throughout the facility, with occupancy sensors in offices, hallways, and restrooms.
3. **Render farm optimization:**
   - Use **render farm scheduling software** (e.g., Deadline, Qube, Tractor) to optimize job scheduling and reduce idle time. Render nodes should be powered off when not in use, rather than left idle.
   - Use **cloud burst rendering** for peak loads (e.g., final VFX delivery crunch), rather than maintaining a large on-premise render farm that is underutilized most of the time. Cloud rendering allows you to scale capacity up and down, paying only for what you use.
   - Select **low-carbon cloud regions** for cloud rendering (see Section 14.1, Data Center Energy Consumption).
4. **Storage optimization:**
   - Implement **tiered storage**: hot storage (SSD) for active projects, warm storage (HDD) for recent projects, cold storage (LTO tape / cloud archive) for archival projects. This reduces the energy consumption of storage by 60–80%.
   - Use **data deduplication** and **compression** to reduce storage requirements. For example, VFX shot versions (v001, v002, v003) often share 90%+ of their pixels. Deduplication can reduce storage by 70–90% for versioned assets.
   - Delete **temporary files** and **cache data** regularly. Render cache, proxy files, and intermediate exports should be purged after project completion.
5. **Sustainable delivery:**
   - Use **digital delivery** (Aspera, Signiant, MASV) for all deliverables (IMF, ProRes, DNxHR). Digital delivery has a carbon footprint of **~0.5 kg CO2e per TB** (for a 1,000 km transfer on a typical grid), while physical media (hard drive shipping) has a footprint of **~50–100 kg CO2e per shipment** (manufacturing + packaging + shipping).
   - If physical media is required (e.g., DCP for cinema delivery), use **reusable hard drives** and **minimal packaging** (cardboard, not plastic). Return drives to the sender for reuse.
   - For DCP delivery, use **satellite or fiber distribution** (e.g., DCP satellite distribution via satellite or fiber network) rather than physical DCP shipping. Satellite distribution has a carbon footprint of **~0.1 kg CO2e per screen**, while physical DCP shipping is **~5–10 kg CO2e per screen** (manufacturing + shipping).

## 14.4 Carbon Offsetting and Environmental Partnerships

### Carbon Offsetting for Unavoidable Emissions

Some emissions are hard to eliminate entirely — facility power, employee commuting, cloud rendering in regions with dirty grids. Carbon offsets can cover the gap, but treat them as a last resort, not a first choice.

**High-Quality Carbon Offsets:**

- **Direct air capture (DAC)** pulls CO2 straight from the atmosphere. It is permanent and verifiable, but expensive at $100–$600 per tonne. Climeworks and Carbon Engineering run commercial plants.
- **Reforestation** is cheaper ($10–$50/tonne) but risky. Forests burn, droughts kill saplings, and the carbon is not locked away forever.
- **Methane capture** from landfills or farms converts a potent greenhouse gas into energy. Effective and cheap ($5–$20/tonne), but additionality can be murky.
- **Renewable energy projects** in developing countries are the cheapest option ($2–$10/tonne), yet verification is often weak and the projects may have happened anyway.

**Recommendation:** For post-production emissions, prioritize **direct air capture** offsets. They are the only option that is truly permanent. If the budget is tight, blend reforestation (short-term cover) with renewable energy (systemic change). Avoid anything under $5/tonne — it is probably non-additional or worse.

**High-Quality Carbon Offsets:**
- **Direct air capture (DAC):** Technology-based offsets that remove CO2 from the atmosphere (e.g., Climeworks, Carbon Engineering). These are expensive ($100–$600/tonne CO2e) but are permanent and verifiable.
- **Reforestation and afforestation:** Planting trees to absorb CO2. These are cheaper ($10–$50/tonne CO2e) but have risks (fires, drought, land use change) and are not permanent (trees die, releasing CO2).
- **Methane capture:** Capturing methane from landfills or agriculture and converting it to energy. These are effective ($5–$20/tonne CO2e) but have additionality risks (would the methane have been captured anyway?).
- **Renewable energy projects:** Funding wind, solar, or hydro projects in developing countries. These are cheap ($2–$10/tonne CO2e) but have additionality and verification challenges.

**Recommendation:** Prioritize **direct air capture** offsets for post-production emissions, as they are permanent and verifiable. If budget is limited, use a mix of **reforestation** (for short-term offset) and **renewable energy** (for long-term systemic change). Avoid cheap offsets ($<5/tonne) that are likely to be non-additional or fraudulent.

### Environmental Partnerships for Post Houses

Post houses can strengthen their credentials by partnering with recognized environmental programs:

- **BAFTA albert:** Certify as an albert supplier. You will need to report your carbon footprint, use renewable energy, and follow sustainable practices. Certified suppliers get listed in the albert directory and are favored by albert productions.
- **Green Production Guide:** Register as a Green Vendor and adopt the guide's best practices. This carries weight with US productions.
- **Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi):** Set concrete reduction targets — for example, 50% by 2030, net-zero by 2050. This is the gold standard for corporate climate commitments.
- **B Corp certification:** Requires rigorous environmental and social assessment. Recognized globally and a strong signal to clients.
- **Local partnerships:** Work with nearby environmental groups — tree-planting, beach clean-ups, renewable energy co-ops — for community engagement and local offsetting.

**What individual DITs can do:**

- Join **albert** as a freelancer. They run a freelancer certification program.
- Follow the **Green Production Guide** freelancer checklist for on-set practices.
- Offset personal travel and gear emissions through high-quality offsets.
- Push for sustainable choices on set: digital dailies, efficient equipment, solar generators where feasible.

- **BAFTA albert:** Become a certified **albert supplier**. This requires carbon footprint reporting, renewable energy use, and sustainable practices. Certified suppliers are listed in the albert directory and are preferred by albert-certified productions.
- **Green Production Guide:** Register as a **Green Vendor** and adopt the Green Production Guide's best practices. This is recognized by US productions.
- **Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi):** Set science-based emission reduction targets (e.g., 50% reduction by 2030, net-zero by 2050). This is the gold standard for corporate climate commitments.
- **B Corp certification:** Become a B Corp, which requires rigorous assessment of environmental and social performance. This is recognized globally and is a strong marketing asset.
- **Local partnerships:** Partner with local environmental organizations (e.g., tree-planting initiatives, beach clean-ups, renewable energy co-ops) for community engagement and offsetting.

**DIT relevance:** Individual DITs can also participate in these partnerships by:
- Joining **albert** as a freelancer (albert has a freelancer certification program).
- Using the **Green Production Guide**'s freelancer checklist for sustainable on-set practices.
- Offsetting personal travel and equipment emissions through high-quality carbon offsets.
- Advocating for sustainable practices on set (e.g., suggesting digital dailies, energy-efficient equipment, renewable energy for generator power).

## 14.5 Future of Sustainable Post-Production — 2026–2030 Outlook

### Near-Term (2026–2027)

By 2027, roughly half the major post houses in North America and Europe are expected to run on 100% renewable energy. Client pressure from Netflix, Amazon, and Disney is driving the shift, and in many regions renewables are now cost-competitive with fossil fuels.

**AV1, VVC, and LCEVC** will become the default for streaming delivery, cutting CDN energy by 30–50% compared to H.264.

Cloud providers are adding carbon dashboards to their consoles. Soon you will be able to see the real-time CO2 cost of every render job and storage tier.

Studio procurement teams are already writing sustainability criteria into vendor contracts. If you want to stay on the approved vendor list, you will need to report your energy mix and reduction plan.

### Mid-Term (2027–2028)

**Carbon labels** on streaming titles may appear — think nutrition labels, but for CO2. A film might carry a tag: "15,000 tonnes CO2e, equivalent to 3,000 round-trip flights London–New York." The EU Digital Services Act could make this mandatory.

Post houses will start treating equipment like a circular resource: refurbish, resell, recycle through ITAD partners instead of binning old gear.

AI will help optimize render scheduling, storage tiering, and cloud region selection to shave off unnecessary energy use. These are decision-support tools, not replacements for common sense.

VP stages will shift to more efficient LED panels (Mini LED with higher luminous efficacy), adaptive brightness that dims for night scenes, and on-site solar or battery storage.

### Long-Term (2028–2030)

Leading post houses may hit net-zero across Scope 1, 2, and 3 by 2030 — through a mix of renewables, efficiency, sustainable sourcing, and high-quality offsets.

Industry standards like ISO 14001 and ISO 14064 could become mandatory for vendors on major productions.

The DIT role may absorb some sustainability coordination. You already manage the most power-hungry gear on set — carts, monitors, storage — so you are the logical person to track and cut that consumption.

AI color tools themselves will get leaner. Smaller, pruned, or quantized neural networks will deliver 90% of the quality for 10% of the energy. "Green AI" is an active research field, and it will matter for anyone running denoise or upscale passes at scale.

- **Renewable energy adoption** in post-production facilities will accelerate, driven by client requirements (Netflix, Amazon, Disney) and cost savings. By 2027, 50% of major post houses in North America and Europe are expected to be on 100% renewable energy.
- **Energy-efficient codecs** (AV1, VVC, LCEVC) will become standard for streaming delivery, reducing CDN energy consumption by 30–50%.
- **Cloud rendering carbon dashboards** will become standard features in AWS, Google Cloud, and Azure, allowing post houses to monitor and optimize the carbon footprint of their cloud workloads in real-time.
- **Sustainable procurement policies** will become standard for major studios and streamers, requiring post vendors to meet sustainability criteria (renewable energy, carbon reporting, waste reduction) to be approved vendors.

### Mid-Term (2027–2028)

- **Carbon labeling** for content delivery may emerge — similar to nutrition labels, streaming platforms may display the carbon footprint of a title (e.g., "This film's production emitted 15,000 tonnes CO2e, equivalent to 3,000 round-trip flights from London to New York"). This would be driven by consumer demand and regulatory pressure (e.g., EU Digital Services Act).
- **Circular economy** practices in post-production — equipment reuse, refurbishment, and recycling programs. Post houses may partner with IT asset disposition (ITAD) companies to ensure that old equipment is refurbished, resold, or recycled responsibly (not dumped in landfills).
- **AI-driven energy optimization** — AI tools that optimize render farm scheduling, storage tiering, and cloud region selection to minimize energy consumption and carbon footprint. These would be decision-support tools for post-production managers.
- **Virtual production sustainability** — VP stages will adopt energy-efficient LED panels (e.g., Mini LED with higher luminous efficacy), adaptive brightness control (reducing brightness for dark scenes), and renewable energy sources (solar panels on stage roofs, battery storage for peak shaving).

### Long-Term (2028–2030)

- **Net-zero post-production** — Leading post houses may achieve net-zero emissions (Scope 1, 2, and 3) by 2030, through a combination of renewable energy, energy efficiency, sustainable sourcing, and high-quality carbon offsets.
- **Sustainable content standards** — Industry-wide standards (e.g., ISO 14001 for environmental management, ISO 14064 for greenhouse gas accounting) may become mandatory for post-production vendors working on major productions.
- **DIT role evolution** — The DIT role may expand to include **sustainability coordination** on set, similar to how some productions now have a "sustainability coordinator" or "green steward." The DIT is already responsible for energy-intensive equipment (DIT cart, monitors, storage), so they are well-positioned to monitor and reduce on-set energy consumption.
- **Green AI** — AI tools for color grading, denoising, and upscaling will be optimized for energy efficiency. For example, smaller neural networks (pruned, quantized, or distilled) that achieve 90% of the quality of large networks with 10% of the energy consumption. This is an active research area in "green AI" or "sustainable AI."
